## Part 3 — House Writing Style

The voice that runs through the strongest GOODProjects submissions:

1. **Mission-anchored openings.** Lead with the mission and the family, not with the organization's history or the grant mechanics.
2. **Whole-family, place-based framing.** Tie every program back to the two-generation, single-neighborhood logic.
3. **Dignity-first vocabulary.** Asset-based language; residents as partners. Avoid deficit framing.
4. **Data-and-heart pairing.** Every statistic is paired with the human reality behind it; every story is grounded by a number.
5. **Specificity as credibility.** Named programs, real outcomes, concrete figures — specificity is what makes a reviewer believe the rest.

These fold together with the **humanize pass (Part 8)**: strong grant prose persuades through specific evidence, not adjectives. Apply both.

## Part 4 — Funder-Type Adaptations

Same canon, different emphasis and compliance load:

**Private foundations.** Lead with mission/vision alignment and the theory of change. Emphasize sustainability and what the partnership makes possible. Lighter compliance; the narrative and the fit do the work.

**Corporate funders.** Connect to the company's CSR priorities, brand, geography, and (where relevant) employee engagement. Expect award caps (often a % of revenue) and a strong local-impact tie. Keep it crisp and outcomes-forward.

**DC government agencies.** Compliance-heavy. Expect: Clean Hands Certificate (MyTax.DC.gov), Certificate of Good Standing, certification forms with a named Authorized Official whose typed name must match the signature, prescribed budget templates, MTDC/indirect treatment, RFA-specific scoring, and a defined scope of work / deliverables. Mirror the RFA's structure and scoring criteria exactly.

**Federal programs.** Heaviest compliance. Expect SAM.gov registration / UEI, federal forms, a negotiated indirect rate, logic models, and real evaluation rigor. **Check entity eligibility hard** — e.g., GOODProjects is *not* a Local Education Agency, so LEA-only programs (McKinney-Vento) are off-limits except as an LEA subcontractor.

**Community foundations.** Local impact and collaboration; often smaller and relationship-driven. Name local partners and concrete neighborhood outcomes.

**Quick reference — typical ranges by funder type** (use for capacity planning at qualification, Part 7, not as a promise to any specific funder):

| Dimension | Federal | Private foundation | Corporate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical award | $500K–$5M+ | $5K–$500K | $5K–$250K |
| Review timeline | 6–9 months | 4–12 weeks | 2–8 weeks |
| Payment | Reimbursement (cash-flow risk — confirm GOODProjects can float costs) | Advance award | Advance award |
| Compliance load | 2 CFR Part 200, SAM.gov/UEI, negotiated indirect rate | Varies, usually light | Minimal |

Federal awards pay on **reimbursement**, not in advance — flag this to Cole Bradshaw at qualification (Part 7) if the program would need to front significant costs before the first federal drawdown.

## Part 5 — Defect List (measured across the corpus)

The systemic quality problems found in the 249 files, in rough order of frequency. The QA gate in Part 6 exists to catch these.

1. **Unfilled budget placeholders** — `[X]`, `[INSERT]`, `TBD`, blanks left in the budget and narrative. Present in **~209 of 249 files (~84%)**. The single most common and most damaging defect.
2. **Leaked machine artifacts** — `internal_name` / `funder_language` tags, underscore slugs, Run IDs, and "Drive-context" appendices left in the body. A clear tell of un-cleaned AI generation.
3. **Contradictory numbers within one submission** — e.g., a headline ask that doesn't match the budget table (the Lubrizol draft asked $250K up top and totaled $25K at the bottom).
4. **Garbled / mismatched framing** — two different project names in one document; wrong or misaligned framework references (e.g., mislabeled SDGs).
5. **Impossible or invalid dates** — completion dates before start dates; malformed years (e.g., `0207`, `0206`); goal dates outside the project period.
6. **Stale staffing boilerplate** — naming the former "Cara Adams, Development Manager." Always replace with the current roster (Part 2).
7. **Forced-fit submissions** — applying to funders with no matching GOODProjects program (e.g., a maternal-health fund). These should be caught at qualification (Part 7), not written.
8. **Google Drive links instead of uploaded attachments** — portals need actual uploaded files; linked Drive docs fail.
9. **Unverified reused figures** — the claims-bank items (Part 2 / Appendix B) pasted forward without checking.
10. **Address / geography inconsistencies** — 994 vs 996 Maine Ave; Ward 6 vs Wards 6 and 8.

## Part 7 — Opportunity Qualification (go/no-go)

Qualify hard *before* drafting. Neo Mangope holds final go/no-go authority; this framework is how to tee up that decision.

Score each opportunity on:

1. **Geographic fit** — does GOODProjects' DC service area satisfy the funder's geographic requirement? *(Lubrizol failed here — it requires a facility in the funder's service area.)*
2. **Program match** — is there a real, current GOODProjects program that addresses the funder's stated priority? If you have to invent the fit, it's a no-go.
3. **Entity eligibility** — is GOODProjects an eligible applicant type? *(McKinney-Vento requires a Local Education Agency — GOODProjects can only participate as an LEA subcontractor.)*
4. **Compliance fit** — can GOODProjects actually meet the funder's procedural load (registration such as SAM.gov/UEI, required certifications, negotiated indirect rate, prescribed templates) inside the timeline? A program that fits the mission but not the compliance machinery is still a no-go.
5. **Capacity & timeline** — is the deadline realistic given the current pipeline?
6. **Strategic value** — renewal, relationship, or strong mission alignment that justifies the effort?

If an opportunity fails 1, 2, 3, or 4, flag it to Neo as a no-go with the reason — do not sink drafting time into it. Selective qualification is the main lever behind a higher award rate.

### Researching a foundation before qualifying it

For any private or corporate foundation opportunity, pull the funder's **IRS Form 990-PF** (via Candid's 990 Finder, ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer, or the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search) before qualifying it. Look at **three to five years**, not one — a single year can be an anomaly. The 990-PF shows: actual past grantees and award amounts (does the funder's real giving match its stated priorities?), the typical award ceiling/floor/average (is GOODProjects' ask in range?), geographic concentration of giving, and board member names. Many foundations — reportedly the large majority — don't publish a full list of grants anywhere else, so the 990-PF is often the only way to see what a funder actually funds versus what its website says it funds.

### Program officer contact

Where the funder allows direct contact, reach out to the Program Officer **before** drafting a major proposal — not to ask "should we apply," but to briefly describe the project and ask whether it aligns with current priorities. This confirms fit before time is spent, and starts the relationship. After any rejection: request reviewer feedback if the funder offers it, thank the funder regardless of outcome, and stay in touch about GOODProjects' work. Treat every submission, funded or not, as one cycle in an ongoing relationship, not a one-off transaction — see Part 11 for the resubmission side of this.

## Part 8 — Humanize Before Export (strip AI-writing tells)

Every GOODProjects draft must pass a **humanize check** before it goes out. The goal: writing that reads like a person who knows the work wrote it — persuading through specific evidence, not adjectives. This complements the house style (Part 3); it does not override the structure the RFA asks for.

> If your tool has a separate style/humanizer reference available, run it too for the full rule set. The condensed, grant-adapted version below is what always applies regardless.

### The loop
1. Draft normally.
2. **Audit:** ask *"what makes this read as AI-generated?"* and list the tells.
3. **Rewrite** to remove them. Then re-scan for em/en dashes — any hit means it is not done.

### What to strip

**Puffery and inflation**
- Significance/legacy inflation: "marks a pivotal moment," "stands as a testament to," "plays a crucial role," "underscores its importance," "evolving landscape." Say what the thing *is* and *does*.
- Promotional / advertisement words: vibrant, groundbreaking, transformative, breathtaking, renowned, nestled, "in the heart of," "boasts a," "commitment to." Grants persuade with evidence, not superlatives.
- Notability padding: listing media coverage or awards without context.

**Fake depth and vagueness**
- Superficial "-ing" tails that add no fact: "highlighting the need for," "ensuring better outcomes," "fostering collaboration," "reflecting a broader…." Cut, or replace with a concrete fact.
- Vague attributions / weasel words: "experts argue," "studies show," "observers note." Grants need *real, named* sources — cite the actual study or agency, or drop the claim.
- Hedging and gap-filling: "while specific details are limited," "it is believed that," "as of [date]." Say what is known, or cut it. Never invent filler to cover a gap — fill the real figure (this is the no-placeholders rule).

**Grammar tells**
- Copula avoidance: prefer "is / are / has" over "serves as / stands as / boasts / features."
- Passive voice and subjectless fragments where active is clearer ("No configuration needed" → "You do not need…").
- Negative parallelisms: "not just X, it's Y," "not only… but also," and tailing negations ("…, no guessing").
- Rule of three: do not force ideas into triads to sound complete; list what is real.
- Synonym cycling: do not rotate "participants / clients / individuals / beneficiaries" to avoid repetition — pick the right word and reuse it.
- False ranges: use "from X to Y" only when X and Y sit on a real scale.

**Formatting tells** (use the RFA's required structure, but cut decorative AI formatting)
- **Em and en dashes: hard cut.** No "—" or "–" anywhere in the final text. Replace with a period, comma, colon, parentheses, or restructure. Also catch spaced em dashes and double hyphens.
- Mechanical boldface and AI-style inline-header bullets ("**Performance:** Performance has been improved…"). Write prose or a clean list instead.
- Title Case In Headings → sentence case. No emojis. Straight quotes, not curly.

**Chatbot artifacts (never leave these in a submission)**
- "Here is an overview…," "I hope this helps," "Certainly!," "Would you like me to…," "Let me know if…." These overlap with the machine-artifact defect (Part 5) — strip them all.

### Grant-specific judgment
- Persuasion is still required; the fix is to persuade with named programs, real numbers, and concrete outcomes (Part 3's "specificity as credibility"), not with adjectives.
- Legitimate enumeration (e.g., a program's three real components) is fine; *forced* triads and empty parallelism are not.
- Keep the headings and structure the RFA requires; cut only decorative or AI-tell formatting.

## Part 9 — Universal Grant Writing Craft Reference

General discipline rules that apply underneath the GOODProjects canon (Part 2) and house style (Part 3). Use this Part when checking a section's structure or wording against best practice generally, not just against past GOODProjects files.

### 9.1 The needs statement — four pillars

Every needs statement should stand on:

1. **Relevance** — the need must map to *this funder's* stated priority and geography, not to poverty/education/food security in general.
2. **Data** — recent, comparative (vs. state/national benchmark or trend), reputable (Census, agency data, recognized research), and cited inline.
3. **Emotion, sparingly** — a story or quote humanizes the data but should not replace it. Roughly 80% data to 20% narrative illustration.
4. **Urgency** — state what happens if the need goes unaddressed now, tied to the data ("Without X, Y population continues to face Z, as evidenced by...").

Avoid circular reasoning: the problem is never "we lack a program" — the problem is the underlying condition (poverty rate, food insecurity, youth violence) that the program would address. Always localize national or state data to the DC service area rather than citing it as if it proves local need on its own.

The Data pillar and the Emotion pillar are executed using the claim-evidence-explanation structure and the logos/ethos/pathos balance in 9.12–9.13 below — a needs statement is where those two subsections matter most.

### 9.2 Goals, objectives, outcomes — SMART(IE)

- **Goal:** broad, long-term aspiration. **Objective:** specific, measurable step toward it. **Output:** direct product of an activity (workshops delivered). **Outcome:** the resulting change (participants' knowledge/behavior/status).
- Every objective should be **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**ime-bound — and where equity is a funder priority, extend to **SMARTIE** (+ **I**nclusive, **E**quitable: designed with community input, addresses systemic barriers).
- Use concrete verbs (increase, decrease, deliver, train, certify) — never fuzzy ones (improve, help, change).
- Number objectives sequentially and place them right after the project description, not buried in prose. Fold the evaluation method into the objective itself where possible: "...as documented by signed intake forms."

### 9.3 Logic model vs. theory of change

- **Logic model:** inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes (short/medium/long-term), usually a table or diagram. Federal and EDGAR-governed funders favor this.
- **Theory of change:** the narrative "we believe that if [population] experiences [intervention], then [change] will occur because [assumption]" — must state the causal "because," not just the sequence.
- Build the logic model first and let it drive the narrative — never retrofit a logic model to match an already-written story. Every SMART(IE) objective should appear somewhere in the outcomes column.

### 9.4 Budget narrative — four mandatory justification points

For every line item:

1. **Show the calculation** — e.g., "3 staff x $75/month office supplies x 12 months = $2,700," not a bare total.
2. **Explain the item** — even routine costs (supplies, mileage) get a sentence; nothing is "self-explanatory."
3. **Tie it to implementation** — connect the expense to the specific activity or outcome it funds.
4. **Include in-kind contributions** as their own justified, valued line items (donated space, volunteer hours), not folded silently into cash totals.

Numbers must reconcile identically across the narrative, the budget summary, and the budget worksheet — see Part 6 QA gate.

### 9.5 Language and grammar discipline

- **Plain language first.** Short, familiar, concrete words; main idea first, then supporting detail. Swap "are dependent upon" → "depend on," "It is our expectation that" → "We expect," "are planned to" → "will."
- **Active voice by default.** "The Project Director will conduct spatial analysis," not "Spatial analysis will be conducted." Passive is acceptable only where a regulation itself is the actor ("Grantees are required to submit quarterly reports under 2 CFR 200.329").
- **Affirmative, declarative commitments.** "Will," not "hope to," "aim to," or "try to."
- **Tense discipline.** Present tense for the org's current, ongoing work and for the problem ("Unemployment remains at..."); future tense, consistently, for what the project will do.
- **One voice, one point of view.** Pick first person ("we") or third person ("GOODProjects") for the whole document and never mix them within a single submission — this is a top-tier stylistic error even when each sentence is individually correct.
- **Mirror the funder's language.** If the RFA asks for "significance," "innovation," or "feasibility," echo those exact words back in the relevant section headers/topic sentences so a rubric-scoring reviewer can find the match instantly.
- **Numbers:** spell out one–nine, use numerals for 10+; always use numerals for percentages, dollars, ages, measurements. Pair every statistic with its comparison point and source ("43% vs. the state average of 82%, FL Dept. of Education 2024").
- **Comparatives need a named reference point** — "25% higher than the national average," never a bare "significantly higher."
- **Punctuation essentials:** Oxford comma in lists; no comma splices (use a period or semicolon between independent clauses); no apostrophes for plurals (CEOs, not CEO's); hyphenate compound modifiers before a noun (evidence-based program).
- **Every acronym spelled out on first use**, capped at roughly three to four acronyms per proposal.
- **Subject-verb agreement.** Watch complex subjects involving the organization, a team, or a data set — a sentence that loses agreement reads as unedited and costs credibility fast.
- **Pronoun reference must be unmistakable.** "It," "they," "this," "these" should point to exactly one noun. Grant sentences juggle the organization, the project, the participants, and the outcomes at once — if a pronoun could point to more than one, name the noun instead.
- **Prefer active verbs over nominalizations.** "Implementation," "utilization," "facilitation" turn actions into bureaucratic nouns. Write "staff will implement," "participants will use," "the case manager will facilitate."
- **Parallel structure in every list, series, and objective set.** "Recruit participants, deliver training, and track completion" — not a mix of gerunds, infinitives, and nouns in the same list. Breaks in parallelism are especially visible in objectives and budget narratives, where reviewers scan and compare line by line.
- **Punctuate for logic, not decoration.** Colons introduce a list or explanation; semicolons connect closely related independent clauses (sparingly); commas separate introductory phrases and list items but never carry a full logical transition between two independent clauses (that is a comma splice — use a period or semicolon instead).
- **Drop hedges in commitment language.** "May," "might," "could," "perhaps" are fine for describing genuine external uncertainty, but never for describing what GOODProjects itself will do. The work plan, methods, and objectives sections need "will," not "may."
- **Every word earns its place.** Cut throat-clearing ("Indeed, it can be argued that..."), meaningless intensifiers ("exciting," "a lot," "really"), and "innovative/passionate" self-labeling — show it with a number instead of asserting it. This is the same discipline as the humanize pass in Part 8; apply both together.

### 9.6 Structural essentials

- **The 20-minute rule.** A reviewer should be able to find problem, solution, capacity, and outcomes within the first page. Section headings should mirror the funder's review criteria.
- **LOIs are written fresh each time** — never pasted from a prior application. One to three pages: opening summary (who/what/how much/how long), need, project activity, anticipated outcomes, credentials, budget total, and a closing request to submit a full proposal. No unsolicited attachments.
- **Executive summary** (300-500 words, 2-4 paragraphs): mission + ask + hook, then problem + solution anchored in data, then the evidence that makes the program extraordinary. Write it last, after the full narrative is done, and close on forward momentum (a partnership invitation), not a summary restatement.
- **Storytelling:** open the needs statement with one real (de-identified or consented) client story, return to that same story in outcomes to show the projected change — never a fabricated composite presented as real.
- **Building on the field, not tearing it down:** frame new approaches as extending existing work ("building on Smith et al. 2023"), not as correcting the field's neglect.

### 9.7 Equity and inclusion language

- Person-first language: "people experiencing homelessness," not "the homeless."
- Lead with community strengths alongside documented needs; avoid deficit-only framing.
- Attribute disparities to systemic conditions, not individual deficits — consistent with the dignity-first, asset-based equity statement in Part 2.
- Where equity is a stated funder priority, use SMARTIE objectives (9.2) and note how community members shaped the program design.

### 9.8 AI-assisted drafting ethics

- Every AI-drafted section gets human review, verification, and revision before it leaves the building — never submit a first-pass AI draft.
- Every data point and statistic gets independently verified against source before it appears in a submission (this is exactly what the ⚠ VERIFY flags in Part 2 / Appendix B exist for).
- AI cannot supply organizational history, client stories, or funder relationships — those still have to come from the team.
- Generic, non-tailored AI boilerplate is detectable by program officers and AI-screening tools alike; customization (Part 4) is the defense.

### 9.9 Style guide default

Follow whatever style guide the RFA/NOFO specifies. When none is specified, default to **APA** for nonprofit submissions (Chicago for general/humanities-leaning funders, GPO conventions for federal). Apply the chosen guide consistently across the entire document once picked.

### 9.10 Sentence and paragraph architecture

Reviewers read under time pressure (see the 20-minute rule, 9.6), so sentence and paragraph form should reduce their cognitive load, not display verbal range.

- **Match sentence length to purpose.** Short sentences for facts, deadlines, and commitments. Longer sentences are fine when showing a real relationship between need, method, and outcome, but they still have to stay grammatically controlled — one relationship per sentence, not three.
- **One claim per paragraph.** A grant paragraph should carry a single need, method, justification, or outcome. Topic sentence states the claim, the middle sentences give evidence or explanation, the closing sentence links forward to the next section's logic. Reviewers frequently score sections separately, so a paragraph that wanders across several ideas wastes the one paragraph a rubric line was going to credit.
- **Cross-sectional coherence check.** Before export, confirm the needs statement, objectives, methods, evaluation plan, and budget are all telling the *same* story — same population, same numbers, same program name. A budget or evaluation section that quietly drifts from the needs statement is one of the fastest ways to lose reviewer trust, even when each section is well written in isolation.

### 9.11 Editorial judgment: novice vs. advanced markers

A few habits separate a proposal that merely completes every section from one that reads as a coherent argument:

- **Interpret data, don't just insert it.** A statistic on its own is not persuasive. State the comparison and say why it matters to this program ("43% vs. the 82% state average, meaning four in ten children enter kindergarten without basic literacy skills GOODLearningHub is built to close").
- **Write to the funder's scoring behavior.** Assume the reviewer is matching text against a rubric, not reading for pleasure — mirror the RFA's own section headers and key terms (9.5) so the match is instant.
- **Edit multi-author drafts into one voice.** When program staff, finance, and leadership each contribute sections, someone has to unify tone, terminology, and sentence rhythm before submission — a proposal that reads like five different people wrote it damages credibility even if every fact in it is correct.
- **Adapt models, never copy them.** Sample proposals, past submissions, and outside templates are useful for structure and logic, not for text. Every submission should be built from GOODProjects' own current facts (Part 2) and this funder's own language (9.5) — not lifted from a prior unrelated proposal or an external template.

### 9.12 Rhetorical balance: logos, ethos, pathos

A proposal that is all data reads as a report; a proposal that is all mission language reads as unfounded opinion. Persuasion is the deliberate balance of three appeals, woven throughout every section rather than walled off into separate ones:

- **Logos** — the logical, evidence-backed argument: statistics, comparative data, program logic. This is Part 2's need statement, Part 9.1's four pillars, and the evaluation plan.
- **Ethos** — the organization's credibility: track record, named staff qualifications, partnerships, prior wins. This is Part 2's staffing roster, partnerships, and claims bank (confirmed wins especially).
- **Pathos** — emotional resonance: a real client story, lived-experience detail, community voice. This is Part 9.6's storytelling guidance — used sparingly and always paired with data, never as a substitute for it.

A section that leans on only one appeal is unfinished. A methods section that is all logos and no ethos reads as generic; a needs statement that is all pathos and no logos reads as unfounded.

### 9.13 Evidence integration: claim, evidence, explanation

The most common defect in evidence use is the **dropped quote or dropped statistic** — a fact inserted with no sentence explaining what it means for this program. Every data point needs a claim to sit inside and an explanation to follow it:

- **Claim** — a declarative sentence stating the argument, not the evidence itself.
- **Evidence** — introduced with a signal phrase, then quoted, paraphrased, summarized, or synthesized (see the four methods below).
- **Explanation** — one sentence, every time, that links the evidence back to the argument and to this program. This is the non-negotiable step: a statistic without an interpretive sentence is not yet doing persuasive work.

*Weak (dropped statistic):* "43% of residents in the service area live below the poverty line (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023)."
*Strong (claim–evidence–explanation):* "Families in the service area face compounding economic instability. The U.S. Census Bureau (2023) documents that 43% of residents live below the poverty line, a rate GOODProjects' two-generation model is built to interrupt by pairing income supports with children's education in the same households."

**Four methods of evidence integration**, in order of complexity:

1. **Quoting** — only when a funder's or agency's exact wording matters (a stated eligibility definition, a funder's own priority language). Keep it short and follow the copyright limits below.
2. **Paraphrasing** — restate a finding in plain language so any reviewer, not just a subject-matter expert, can follow it.
3. **Summarizing** — condense a longer report into a sentence or two for the needs statement; do not reproduce its structure.
4. **Synthesizing** — the highest-value method: combine two or three sources to show convergence rather than repeating one. E.g., "Census data on poverty, Department of Labor data on unemployment, and Jones et al. (2022) on housing instability all point to the same compounding conditions in the service area." Reserve synthesis for the needs statement's strongest paragraph — it does more persuasive work than any single statistic.

**Signal phrases carry precision, not just transition.** Match the verb to the strength of the source: "confirms," "documents," "demonstrates" for strong, primary evidence; "suggests," "indicates" only where the source itself is tentative. This replaces the vague-attribution tell in Part 8 ("studies show," "experts argue") with a named source and a verb that matches its actual certainty.

**Before any source is used, check it against five criteria:** relevant to the specific claim being made, accurate against the primary source (not a secondary retelling), credible (government, peer-reviewed, or institutionally recognized), timely (generally within five years unless historical context is the point), and — across the needs statement as a whole — diverse in type (statistical, policy, and qualitative sources together, not statistics alone).

Note on quoting funder or source language in a submission: keep any direct quotation short and attributed, per the same copyright discipline that applies to any external material — this playbook is not a license to reproduce a funder's or a researcher's text at length.

## Part 10 — Post-Award Compliance and Reporting (federal awards)

Winning is not the finish line. A grant the organization cannot manage and report on correctly damages GOODProjects' credibility with that funder and, often, with others watching the same field — so this belongs in the same file as drafting, not a separate finance-only document.

**Reporting cadence (2 CFR §200.329):** federal performance reports are due no less than annually and no more than quarterly, at whatever interval the awarding agency sets. Annual reports are due within 90 days of the reporting period; final performance reports within 120 days of the period-of-performance end date.

**Single Audit threshold:** an organization expending **$1,000,000 or more** in federal awards in a fiscal year must undergo a Single Audit (2 CFR §200.501). Flag this to Cole Bradshaw whenever a federal award, combined with existing federal funding, could cross that line.

**Record retention:** federal grant records must be kept **3 years from the date of the final expenditure report**, longer if litigation, a claim, or an audit is active (2 CFR §200.334).

**Five compliance failures to watch for** (the most common causes of federal grant trouble, and the reason the QA gate in Part 6 exists):
1. Restricted funds and general accounting kept in disconnected systems, so allocations drift.
2. Weak time-and-effort documentation for staff charged to the grant — usually the largest line item.
3. Missed reporting deadlines from manual, undocumented tracking.
4. Unallowable costs — expenses that aren't necessary, reasonable, or allocable to that specific award.
5. Insufficient monitoring of any subrecipient GOODProjects passes federal funds through to.

Any federal budget or work plan that doesn't have a realistic answer for who tracks time-and-effort, who owns the reporting calendar, and who monitors any subrecipients is not ready to submit — raise it at qualification (Part 7's compliance-fit check), not after the award lands.

## Part 11 — Rejection and Resubmission Strategy

Rejection is the normal outcome, not a sign of a bad proposal. Typical success rates run roughly: federal grants 10–25%, foundation grants 15–30%, highly competitive national programs 5–10%, local/community grants 30–50%. Set that expectation with the team up front so a decline reads as ordinary pipeline math, not proof the proposal failed.

**After any rejection, work the decision in order:** read the reviewer comments/scores if provided; decide whether the noted weaknesses are actually correctable; loop in the relevant program lead; draft a specific response to each criticism; then decide whether to resubmit to the same funder, redirect to a better-fit funder, or shelve the project idea.

**Funder-specific resubmission rules vary — check before assuming a simple resubmit is welcome:**
- Some federal agencies (e.g., NIH-style review processes) expect a short, explicit response to prior reviewer criticism in the resubmission rather than a silently revised document.
- Others (e.g., NSF-style review) treat every resubmission as a brand-new proposal — merely patching the noted deficiencies does not guarantee a better score, so the whole proposal needs to be strong again, not just the flagged section.
- Always confirm the specific agency's resubmission policy rather than assuming one federal funder's rule applies to another.

**Foundation resubmission is relational, not procedural:** a brief, gracious thank-you note, a request for feedback where the funder allows it, and a clear expression of continued interest — this is what Part 7's program-officer relationship work is for.

**Common correctable weaknesses to check before resubmitting anywhere** (cross-check against Part 5's defect list and the Part 6 QA gate): thin or non-comparative data in the needs statement, vague methods about who does what and when, unrealistic timelines given actual staffing, budget lines that don't match the narrative, a capacity section that doesn't show real prior performance, and technical errors (page limits, fonts, missing attachments) that should never reach a reviewer in the first place.
